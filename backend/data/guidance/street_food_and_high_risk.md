## Choosing street food

Pick stalls that are busy, look clean, keep food covered and cook it fresh in front of you.
Freshly fried or boiled food served piping hot is the safer choice. Be careful with items that
are not cooked after handling: pani-puri water, fresh chutneys, raw salads, cut fruit, and
drinks with ice or water of unknown origin. These carry the most risk during the monsoon.

## Signs of food poisoning

Food poisoning usually causes nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps and diarrhoea, starting a few
hours to a few days after eating. Most cases get better on their own within a day or two.
Drink plenty of safe fluids, such as ORS, to replace lost water and salts. Seek medical care
for signs of dehydration (very little urine, dizziness, a dry mouth), blood in stool or vomit,
a high fever, symptoms lasting more than 2–3 days, or if the person is a young child, pregnant,
elderly or has a weak immune system.

## Pregnancy

Pregnant women are more vulnerable to some food-borne infections, such as listeriosis, which
can harm the baby. They should avoid unpasteurised (unboiled) milk and soft cheeses made from
it, raw or undercooked eggs, meat, chicken and fish, and leftovers that are not reheated until
steaming hot. Paneer made from boiled or pasteurised milk and cooked through is a safer choice.

## Infants and young children

Children's immune systems are still developing, so their food should be freshly prepared and
served soon after cooking. Do not give honey to babies under 1 year old, because it can cause
infant botulism. Use boiled and cooled water for feeds, clean feeding bottles and utensils
thoroughly, and throw away leftover milk or food from a feed.

## Older adults and weak immunity

Older adults and people with diabetes, cancer, kidney disease or weak immunity get sicker from
food-borne illness. They should eat food that is freshly cooked and thoroughly heated, avoid
raw or undercooked eggs, meat and seafood, and be especially strict about leftovers and
refrigeration.
